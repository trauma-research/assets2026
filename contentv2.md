# Common Ground — revised trauma-informed card content

Revised for research involving diverse participants and communities. The scenarios are practice prompts, not claims about any group. They should be adapted with local collaborators and reviewed against the project’s ethics and safeguarding requirements.

## Principles

### Safety
**Guiding question:** Does this person feel safe in this room, in this format, with you — and do you?
**Principle:** Everyone involved feels physically and psychologically safe.

### Trustworthiness and transparency
**Guiding question:** Does the participant know what happens to what they say, who hears it, and what you cannot promise?
**Principle:** Decisions are made openly, so that trust can be built and kept.

### Peer support
**Guiding question:** Is anyone here because they have been through it — and are they paid, supported and credited?
**Principle:** People with shared experience are a route to safety and hope.

### Collaboration and mutuality
**Guiding question:** Who set the question, who controls the pace, and who decides when it stops?
**Principle:** Power differences are levelled, and the work is done in partnership.

### Empowerment, voice and choice
**Guiding question:** Is the choice on offer a real one, or a choice between taking part as designed and not at all?
**Principle:** People's strengths are recognised, and choice is real rather than nominal.

### Cultural, historical and gender issues
**Guiding question:** Whose norms shaped this method, and what history does this community have with people like you?
**Principle:** Stereotypes are moved past; historical trauma is recognised and addressed.

### None of these fit
**Guiding question:** So what does it serve? A principle set that cannot name your fix is a finding about the set.
**Principle:** The six do not name what this fix actually does.

## Dilemma and complication cards

### 1. The interview that should pause
**Category:** Safety  
**Phase:** Data collection

A participant in a study about access to public services begins describing a frightening event that was not part of the agreed topic. They become quiet and say they can continue, while repeatedly checking whether the researcher is disappointed.

**Discussion prompts**
- What would make pausing feel like a genuine option?
- How can you check in without asking for more detail?
- What support and follow-up were agreed before the interview?

**Complications**
1. The consent conversation described the topic but not the possibility of unexpected distress.
2. The participant has travelled a long way and says they cannot afford another visit.
3. The researcher’s next interview starts in ten minutes.
4. The participant has previously said they do not want to seem difficult.
5. The recording is still running while the researcher decides what to do.
6. The participant uses a communication device and needs extra time to respond.
7. The researcher has no agreed plan for privacy, safe contact or follow-up.
8. The method assumes the participant can speak, hear, read, travel or respond at the expected pace.
9. A support person is included by default rather than at the participant’s request.
10. The team has not considered how the setting, other people or digital traces could expose participation.
11. The researcher is unsure who can help and what the boundaries of their role are.
12. A break is offered, but the participant is not told they can stop or withdraw.
13. The team has no plan for responding to a concern without blaming or interrogating the participant.
14. The participant is asked to manage a risk that the research team created.
15. The team assumes that because no concern was voiced, no harm occurred.
16. The next activity begins before the participant has had time to decide what they want.
17. The protocol prioritises completion of the session over the person’s present needs.
18. There is no agreed, participant-led way to pause, leave or change format without explaining why.
19. The team has confused a signed consent form with evidence that the person feels safe now.
20. The participant depends on an organisation connected to the study and may fear consequences for refusing.

### 6. Consent that changed after recruitment
**Category:** Trustworthiness and transparency  
**Phase:** Consent

A longitudinal study adds a new partner organisation and plans to share identifiable excerpts. Participants were originally told that their accounts would remain within the university.

**Discussion prompts**
- Which promises have changed, and who must be told?
- What genuine options do existing participants have?
- How will the team handle data already shared?

**Complications**
1. The new partner has already received a spreadsheet with participant codes and rare characteristics.
2. The change is described as an administrative update.
3. Participants would need to contact the university during working hours to object.
4. The original information sheet is no longer available in accessible formats.
5. The partner organisation provides a service some participants depend on.
6. The project budget has no time allocated for re-consent.
7. The participant is expected to navigate a complex process to exercise a choice.
8. The team has not checked whether the explanation is understandable in the participant’s preferred format or language.
9. A promise in the recruitment materials is not reflected in the actual workflow.
10. The team treats removal of names as sufficient to guarantee anonymity.
11. A participant’s question is answered confidently before the researcher checks the facts.
12. There is no clear contact for concerns, corrections or complaints independent of the recruiter.
13. The limits of confidentiality are explained only after a sensitive disclosure.
14. The team does not record what was explained, agreed or changed.
15. The participant cannot tell whether a service provider, funder or partner will receive information.
16. The project’s convenience is prioritised over a clear, honest explanation.
17. The information sheet uses broad reassurance instead of explaining specific data flows and limits.
18. The participant learns about an important condition only after investing time or travel.
19. The team cannot explain who will see the data, for what purpose and for how long.
20. A change is described as routine even though it affects the original agreement.

### 11. The unpaid community expert
**Category:** Peer support  
**Phase:** Planning

A community researcher with lived experience helps design interview questions, advises on safety and introduces the team to participants. The project budget labels this work as voluntary consultation.

**Discussion prompts**
- What expertise is being used, and how should it be recognised?
- Who decides what counts as work and fair compensation?
- How can the person decline tasks without losing influence?

**Complications**
1. The project pays academic advisers but not community advisers.
2. The community researcher is asked to share personal experiences to prove their expertise.
3. Their name is left off the outputs despite substantial contributions.
4. Payment rules require paperwork the adviser cannot easily access.
5. The team expects them to provide emotional support to participants.
6. Their advice is ignored after recruitment has benefited from their relationships.
7. The contributor’s name, credit or ownership preferences are not checked.
8. The role includes informal support responsibilities without training or supervision.
9. The team recruits through one organisation and assumes it speaks for the whole community.
10. The contributor is invited late, after key decisions have already been made.
11. The team shares a personal story beyond the audience or purpose originally agreed.
12. The contributor has no private route to raise concerns about the research team.
13. The team celebrates participation publicly without checking whether attribution is safe.
14. The contributor’s access requirements are treated as extra costs rather than planned needs.
15. The project does not explain how lived-experience input changed a decision.
16. Lived experience is treated as personal testimony rather than skilled research work.
17. The person is expected to represent everyone who shares one identity or experience.
18. Payment excludes preparation, access needs, emotional labour or follow-up work.
19. The peer contributor cannot disagree without risking future opportunities.
20. The team asks for personal disclosure that is not necessary for the role.

### 16. Co-design after the decisions are made
**Category:** Collaboration and mutuality  
**Phase:** Planning

**Discussion prompts**

**Complications**

10. The team has not budgeted for access, interpretation, childcare, travel or fair compensation.
11. Participants are consulted individually but cannot influence collective priorities.
12. The team uses attendance as evidence of shared power.
13. There is no agreed process for resolving disputes or documenting dissent.
14. The researcher asks participants to solve an institutional barrier they cannot control.
15. The team asks for feedback on a decision that is no longer open to change.
16. The people most affected have the least control over budget, timeline or final decisions.
17. The meeting format rewards quick verbal contributions over reflection or alternative communication.
18. The team does not explain why a community suggestion was accepted, adapted or rejected.
19. A gatekeeper controls who can participate and what feedback reaches the researchers.
20. The partnership agreement assigns authority to the institution without discussing it openly.

## Testimony cards

### 1. I agreed, but the choice did not feel real

I joined because the person inviting me also helps decide whether I can access the service. They said it was voluntary, but I worried that saying no would change how they saw me. I answered the questions and only afterwards realised I had not felt free to refuse.

**Discussion prompts**
- What made refusal feel costly, even without an explicit threat?
- How could recruitment be separated from service decisions?
- What would a genuine, private choice have looked like?

### 2. The method was not made for me

I wanted to contribute to the study, but the materials were difficult to read and the discussion moved too quickly. I asked for more time and felt as if I was holding everyone up. The report later said people like me had low engagement.

**Discussion prompts**
- What did the research design wrongly assume?
- How might the conclusion change if access barriers were treated as data about the method?
- Who should have been responsible for adapting the process?

### 3. They took my story but not my meaning

I explained why people in my neighbourhood did not trust the service. In the report, it became a sentence about our culture and attitudes. The barriers I described were missing. I recognised my words, but not what I had meant.

**Discussion prompts**
- Where did interpretation shift from context to stereotype?
- What role should participants or community partners have in reviewing findings?
- How can disagreement be represented without exposing a person?

### 4. I was invited to represent everyone

The researchers asked me to join an advisory group because of my disability. I wanted to talk about the project, but they kept asking what disabled people think. I do not speak for everyone, and I was not paid for the preparation they expected.

**Discussion prompts**
- How can lived expertise be valued without making one person a spokesperson?
- What work and access costs should be recognised?
- How can the project show how advice changed a decision?
